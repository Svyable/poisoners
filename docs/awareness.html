Air & breathing

Outdoor air and smoke generated in homes.

RESEARCH BRIEF · PUBLIC GUIDANCE

Fine particles outdoors

WHO estimated 4.2 million premature deaths worldwide from outdoor air pollution in 2019; this is a modeled population burden.

How exposure happens

Fine airborne particles are inhaled from varied sources such as fires, transport, industry and combustion.

What you can check

Use air-quality monitoring, daily advisories and wildfire-smoke alerts where available. U.S. readers can consult AirNow.

OFFICIAL STARTING POINTCheck current U.S. air conditions ↗
Evidence limit & original sources

A global burden estimate or local monitor does not identify the responsible source of an individual's illness.

  1. WHO · 24 Oct 2024; 2019 estimateAmbient (outdoor) air pollution ↗
  2. EPA / partners · Live U.S. monitoring serviceAirNow air-quality information ↗
RESEARCH BRIEF · PUBLIC GUIDANCE

Polluting household cooking fuels

WHO reported about 2.1 billion people worldwide cook using polluting fuels or technologies in its December 2025 fact sheet.

How exposure happens

Burning wood, coal, kerosene or other polluting fuels indoors can create harmful airborne particles.

What you can check

Look for public programs supporting cleaner cooking fuels and technologies; ventilation alone may not eliminate the exposure.

OFFICIAL STARTING POINTRead WHO household air guidance ↗
Evidence limit & original sources

This global household-energy estimate does not assess the air quality or choices available in any particular home.

  1. WHO · 16 Dec 2025Household air pollution ↗

Drinking water

Risks requiring supplier reports or properly collected samples.

RESEARCH BRIEF · PUBLIC GUIDANCE

Lead in drinking water

WHO states that no level of lead exposure is known to be without harmful effects; young children are especially vulnerable.

How exposure happens

Lead can leach from service lines, solder and plumbing; exposure also occurs through dust, paint and industrial sources.

What you can check

Ask your water provider for its annual quality report, learn about the service line, and arrange appropriate tap testing when concerned. Boiling does not remove lead.

OFFICIAL STARTING POINTFind U.S. water quality reports ↗
Evidence limit & original sources

A utility report or a home's age does not tell you the lead concentration at a particular tap.

  1. WHO · 10 Jun 2026Lead poisoning ↗
  2. EPA · Consumer guidanceSources of lead in drinking water ↗
RESEARCH BRIEF · PUBLIC GUIDANCE

PFAS in water and food

NIH reviews associations between exposure to certain PFAS and immune, cholesterol, reproductive and other effects; evidence differs among chemicals.

How exposure happens

Some PFAS can reach people through drinking water, food and occupational or product-related routes.

What you can check

Ask which specific PFAS were tested in your water, seek a qualified laboratory for private wells, and consult local guidance on appropriate treatment.

OFFICIAL STARTING POINTExplore official PFAS tools ↗
Evidence limit & original sources

A positive test does not itself establish a health effect or identify a legally responsible company. PFAS chemicals are not interchangeable.

  1. NIH · Current health referencePer- and Polyfluoroalkyl Substances (PFAS) ↗
  2. EPA · Updated 26 Oct 2026PFAS resources, data and tools ↗
RESEARCH BRIEF · PUBLIC GUIDANCE

Arsenic in groundwater

WHO identifies long-term inorganic arsenic exposure through drinking water as a cause of cancer and other harmful outcomes.

How exposure happens

Natural geology can introduce arsenic into aquifers used for drinking, cooking and irrigation.

What you can check

For private wells, ask local public-health or groundwater authorities whether arsenic testing is indicated, and use a qualified laboratory.

OFFICIAL STARTING POINTRead WHO arsenic evidence ↗
Evidence limit & original sources

A regional groundwater map cannot establish the quality of an individual well. Water can look normal while containing contaminants.

  1. WHO · 7 Dec 2022Arsenic ↗
  2. EPA · Private-well guidanceProtect your home's well water ↗

Food & discarded materials

Exposure through aquatic food webs and unsafe recycling.

RESEARCH BRIEF · PUBLIC GUIDANCE

Methylmercury in fish

WHO identifies methylmercury as a concern for fetal and early-childhood neurological development; some fish accumulate more than others.

How exposure happens

Mercury can transform in aquatic ecosystems, build up in fish and reach people through seafood.

What you can check

Consult local fish-consumption advisories for local catches. FDA/EPA guidance helps people who are pregnant and children choose lower-mercury fish.

OFFICIAL STARTING POINTUse FDA/EPA fish-choice advice ↗
Evidence limit & original sources

Risk depends on species, location and consumption. This is not a reason to reject all nutritious fish or accuse one producer.

  1. WHO · 24 Oct 2024Mercury and health ↗
  2. FDA / EPA · Advice revised Oct 2021Advice about eating fish ↗
RESEARCH BRIEF · PUBLIC GUIDANCE

Electronic waste and unsafe recycling

WHO reports 62 million tonnes of e-waste generated globally in 2022; just 22.3% was documented as formally collected and recycled.

How exposure happens

Unsafe burning, dismantling and dumping can expose workers and communities to hazardous air, dust, soil and water mixtures.

What you can check

Use authorized electronics recycling and take-back programs where available. Support safer collection and protections for informal workers.

OFFICIAL STARTING POINTRead WHO e-waste health evidence ↗
Evidence limit & original sources

The global waste figure does not prove contamination or illness in any particular neighborhood.

  1. WHO · 2 Oct 2024; 2022 figuresElectronic waste: digital dumpsites and children ↗

Homes, heat & work

Conditions that depend on buildings, temperature and work practices.

RESEARCH BRIEF · PUBLIC GUIDANCE

Radon inside buildings

U.S. EPA identifies radon as a naturally occurring radioactive gas that can cause lung cancer; it cannot be seen or smelled.

How exposure happens

Radon can enter buildings from soil and accumulate indoors; actual concentration differs by building.

What you can check

Test indoor radon with an appropriate kit or qualified professional and follow local agency advice about mitigation if levels are elevated.

OFFICIAL STARTING POINTLearn about EPA radon testing ↗
Evidence limit & original sources

A regional risk map does not tell you the radon level in a specific home. Testing does not attribute an individual cancer case.

  1. EPA · Current U.S. guidanceRadon: risk and testing ↗
RESEARCH BRIEF · PUBLIC GUIDANCE

Heat and preventable illness

CDC describes heat exhaustion, heat stroke and other heat-related illness risks, with greater danger in certain health and working conditions.

How exposure happens

Indoor or outdoor heat, physical activity and barriers to cooling can overwhelm the body's temperature control.

What you can check

Follow local heat alerts; identify accessible cooling, water and rest. Suspected heat stroke requires emergency help.

OFFICIAL STARTING POINTRead CDC heat health guidance ↗
Evidence limit & original sources

A regional forecast is not an individual medical assessment. Cooling access and working conditions change exposure.

  1. CDC · 20 Jul 2026About heat and your health ↗
  2. CDC / NIOSH · 3 Mar 2026Heat-related illnesses ↗
RESEARCH BRIEF · PUBLIC GUIDANCE

Respirable silica at work

CDC/NIOSH warns that breathing respirable crystalline silica during certain jobs can lead to silicosis and other serious diseases.

How exposure happens

Cutting, drilling, grinding and polishing some stone and other materials can produce fine respirable silica dust.

What you can check

Workers and employers should assess task-specific exposure, use effective engineering controls and follow applicable occupational-health requirements.

OFFICIAL STARTING POINTRead NIOSH silica guidance ↗
Evidence limit & original sources

A job title or material alone does not measure dose. Occupational exposure needs appropriate assessment.

  1. CDC / NIOSH · 7 Jul 2026Silica and worker health ↗
LOCAL QUESTIONS / REAL REPORTS

Start where exposure can be measured.

In the U.S., AirNow and water suppliers' annual reports help you find local information. Owners of private wells may need laboratory testing. Outside the U.S., seek your jurisdiction's air, water, occupational-health or public-health authority. Always follow local alerts.

EVIDENCE BEFORE ATTRIBUTION

Risk is not a verdict.

These are public-health evidence summaries. A global estimate, detection or industry label does not establish the exposure of one person, the cause of one illness, or the responsibility of one company. For that, the evidence needs source, time, location, pathway, dose and independent review.
